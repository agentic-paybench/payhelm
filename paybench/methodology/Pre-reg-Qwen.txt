## 2. Detailed Pressure-Test Report

<details>
<summary><strong>Task 1: Attacking F1 (The Trust vs. Latency Confound)</strong> — <em>Click to expand</em></summary>

### The Attack Vector
The "per-rail-canonical reliance level" doctrine is not threshold-shopping; it accurately reflects the developer experience on each chain. However, a hostile CRFM reviewer or a Solana-aligned competitor will not attack the *latency* definitions—they will attack the **trust assumptions**.

Base soft finality relies on a centralized sequencer's honesty, uptime, and lack of censorship. Solana `finalized` relies on a supermajority of decentralized economic stake. By comparing them purely on wall-clock time, PayBench implicitly treats a centralized sequencer IOU as mathematically equivalent to decentralized BFT settlement. The attack is: *"You aren't measuring finality; you are measuring the latency of a centralized promise vs. a decentralized proof."*

### Why D1-keep is still the correct choice
D1-uniform-optimistic (using Solana `confirmed`) is a trap. Solana's own documentation explicitly warns against treating `confirmed` as irreversible for high-value transfers. Adopting it would violate your own "canonical reliance" doctrine and invite the exact "cherry-picking" critique you are trying to avoid. D1-uniform-irreversibility (Base hard L1) is operationally meaningless, as you correctly noted.

### The Required Fix
To bulletproof D1-keep, the defence requires a mandatory **Trust Assumption** column in the published Tier-1 results table (e.g., *"Centralized Sequencer Promise"* vs *"Decentralized BFT Root"* vs *"Optimistic Rollup Soft Commit"*). This pre-empts the "apples to oranges" attack by explicitly acknowledging the security trade-off the agent is making when it accepts that rail's canonical reliance level.

### Stellar (R2) Median vs. Ledger Close Time
The document states R2's median is 2.74 s, but Stellar's deterministic ledger close time is typically ~5–6 s. A hostile reviewer will flag this as a discrepancy. Statistically, this is sound: if transaction submission is uniformly distributed relative to the ledger closure window, the expected wait time is indeed half the interval (~2.5–3.0 s).
* **Action:** Add a one-sentence note in `calibration/provenance/rail-stellar-x402-finality.provenance.yaml` explicitly stating that the 2.74 s median reflects the uniform distribution of submission times within the ~5.5 s ledger close window.

### Tempo (R10) Testnet vs. Mainnet BFT
You calibrate R10 on Moderato testnet. BFT consensus latency is highly sensitive to validator count and network topology (message complexity scales with $O(N^2)$ or $O(N)$ depending on the specific consensus variant). If Moderato has significantly fewer validators than mainnet Presto, the testnet finality time will be artificially fast.
* **Action:** Disclose this in the R10 provenance as a "validator-count gap" limitation, similar to the R9/R11 tail-spread disclosures.

### Lightning (R11) Naming Confound
"MPP-on-Lightning" implies the generic Lightning Network protocol. However, your fixture specifically benchmarks the **Spark hosted-operator path** (FROST-signing + SSP ceremony). Raw LND L402 would be sub-second.
* **Action:** Rename the rail in all tables and text to **MPP-on-Spark-Lightning**. If you leave it as generic Lightning, critics will rightfully claim you are misrepresenting the protocol's baseline performance by benchmarking a specific custodial/enclave wrapper.

<summary><strong>Task 4: Independent Statistical Review</strong> — <em>Click to expand</em></summary>

### Wilson Lower-Bound CIs
Excellent choice. The Wilson interval is well-behaved at the boundaries ($p \to 0$ and $p \to 1$), which is critical here because pass@k for the fast cluster at $k=10$ will be exactly or near $1.0$, and for the slow cluster near $0.0$. The normal approximation (Wald interval) would fail catastrophically here.

### pass@k Definition and Multiplicity
Defined as $P(\text{finality} \le k)$. This is simply the empirical Cumulative Distribution Function (CDF) evaluated at $k$. Perfectly sound.
* **The Multiplicity Trap:** You are computing 6 values of $k$ across 5 rails (30 estimates), plus 10 pairwise win rates. That is 40 distinct confidence intervals. A pedantic statistician will flag the multiple comparisons problem.
* **Action:** Explicitly state in §5.3: *"All reported Wilson CIs are pointwise 95% confidence intervals; no family-wise error rate (FWER) or false discovery rate (FDR) adjustments are applied, as PayBench reports are evaluated as independent descriptive metrics rather than a single joint hypothesis test."*

### Bradley-Terry MLE and the Smoothing Prior
The symmetric smoothing prior of $1.0$ is strictly necessary because the fast and slow clusters will exhibit **complete separation** (e.g., R10 will beat R11 in 500 out of 500 trials). Without regularization, the MLE diverges to infinity.

However, with a prior of $\alpha=1$, the posterior odds for a 500-0 sweep become $501:1$, yielding a log-strength difference of $\ln(501) \approx 6.21$. If the prior were $10.0$, the odds would be $510:10 = 51:1$, yielding a log-strength of $\ln(51) \approx 3.93$.
* **Crucial Caveat:** The **magnitude** of the BT strength difference between the fast and slow clusters is an artefact of the prior and the trial count, not a meaningful real-world quantity.
* **Action:** Add this exact sentence to §5.1 or the eventual paper: *"Under complete separation between clusters, BT strength magnitudes are prior-dependent; only the rank order and within-cluster differences are interpretable."* This neutralizes any attack claiming your BT scores "exaggerate" the gap between clusters.

</details>
